GRH via exercises

We present the arguments of Alpöge's proof of the generalized Riemann hypothesis for Dirichlet $L$-functions as a series of exercise sheets, each introducing one ingredient in its simplest setting. The sheets were assembled from the Lean code and from the expositions listed under Sources below. The presentation is inspired by the problem sequences of Onishchik and Vinberg, Lie Groups and Algebraic Groups (Springer, 1990). The sheets are drafts written and refereed by AI agents (Claude and Codex).

How the sheets fit together. Parts I–V treat each ingredient in its simplest setting. The later parts state the ingredients in the generality the proof uses, drawing on the Lean code and the expositions under Sources, and the last part assembles them. The later parts are under construction: their chapters were drafted from the Lean code, and an audit of their statements against the Lean declarations is in progress, so for now they may misstate hypotheses or attributions. A flowchart shows how the chapters depend on each other, with sample seminar plans.

Notation. Each sheet defines what it uses. A few letters recur throughout: \(p\) is a prime, usually inert in \(K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-d})\) (or \(K = \mathbb{Q}(i)\) in the simplest cases), and \(q\), \(d\) are the auxiliary primes; \(G(x) = \sum_n \Lambda(n)(x/n - 1)_+ - x\log x + (1 + \gamma)x\) is the ramp sum less its main term, \(G_\chi\) its twist by \(\chi\), and \(c_\chi = \frac{L'}{L}(1, \chi)\); \(\psi\) is the character whose $L$-function is treated. In the later chapters \(E \supset K\) is a CM field, while in the chapters on elliptic curves \(E\) is an elliptic curve.

Part I. The target

  • 1. From zeros to a one-sided ramp sum
    Reduce the Riemann hypothesis for a Dirichlet $L$-function to an upper bound for a one-sided ramp sum over primes.
  • 2. Inert primes and Estimate B
    Recover the ramp sum from ramp sums over odd powers of primes inert in an imaginary quadratic field.
  • 3. Real characters over prime moduli
    Estimate B for the first auxiliary prime, from mean squares over prime moduli, Gallagher's density estimate and Page's theorem; what must be repeated for the second.
  • 4. Fourier analysis on the Galois group
    Expand weighted prime sums over the places of an abelian CM field in characters, and average over translates.
  • 5. Columns from a sphere
    How the distinguished columns are chosen: Behrend's construction gives a dense set that meets every short progression at most twice.

Part II. The method

  • 6. The slope method
    Bound a sum over primes by comparing divisibility with size, from Chebyshev to Bost's slope inequality.
  • 7. The slope inequality in the mean
    Use many maps into one target to replace its maximal slope by its mean slope.
  • 8. The chain and the square root
    Stepanov's method for \(y^2 = f(x)\), and a chain of maps between lattices in place of an order of vanishing.
  • 9. Stepanov without multiplicity
    What the size and divisibility of one determinant say about primes, and about Siegel zeros.
  • 10. The rank lemma and the zero estimate
    The rank hypothesis of the mean slope inequality from Gram matrices, equidistribution and a relative trace formula.
  • 11. Poincaré series and near orthogonality
    The rank hypothesis as in the argument, from the Petersson formula and the decay of Bessel functions.

Part III. Divisibility at inert primes

  • 12. Complex tori, theta functions and periods
    Weierstrass and theta functions on the lemniscatic curve, and its period.
  • 13. The Fock model and theta intertwiners
    Theta functions of a lattice form a line; intertwiners between lattices compose up to a Gauss sum.
  • 14. Complex multiplication and reduction at an inert prime
    Formal groups with complex multiplication and the derivation \(D^p\) at a supersingular prime.
  • 15. One CM determinant: Mertens modulo 4
    How much of \(\log r!\) one determinant of CM values recovers from each prime.
  • 16. The invariant derivation at a supersingular prime
    How divisible the derivatives of a lemniscatic theta function are at an inert prime, in one variable.
  • 17. The isotropic plane
    A second variable, along the isotropic eigenlines, gives a gain at every prime \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 8\).
  • 18. Pairings on a formal group of height two
    Why the gain at an inert prime is \(\alpha p/(p^2-1)\): Frobenius-semilinear pairings on the Lubin–Tate group and their normal form.
  • 19. Hecke nondegeneracy on the tree
    The loss that balances the gain: elementary divisors of a Hecke operator on symmetric powers, by Kummer's theorem.

Part IV. Sizes, periods and counts

  • 20. The Kronecker limit formula and Chowla–Selberg
    Kronecker's limit formula and Lerch's formula compute \(L'(1,\chi)/L(1,\chi)\) in two ways, and \(e^{\gamma - L'(1,\chi)/L(1,\chi)}\) is, up to an algebraic factor, the square of a CM period divided by \(2\pi i\).
  • 21. Masses and the count of forms
    The number of forms on a definite unitary group, as a mass times a Weyl dimension.
  • 22. The Siegel–Weil formula in the smallest case
    The mass-weighted average of theta series over a genus is an Eisenstein series, for \(E_8\), rank 16 and the class set of Masses and the count of forms.
  • 23. Definite theta lifts and their norms
    The entries and sizes of theta lifts between definite unitary groups in the smallest cases.
  • 24. Hecke operators and theta lifts
    Eichler's commutation relation, $p$-neighbours and Brandt matrices.
  • 25. Divisibility along the chain
    Carrying gains along a chain of lifts by inverting the one-step Hecke relation, and what it costs.
  • 26. Covolumes of lattices of sections
    Exact covolumes for polynomials and for forms on a definite group, against the arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel theorem.
  • 27. Chow forms and an effective Hilbert–Samuel bound
    A lower bound for the covolume of sections from the Chow form and Hadamard's inequality.
  • 28. Covolumes and periods
    How a period takes the place of the self-intersection for theta functions on an elliptic curve.

Part V. Denominators and black boxes

  • 29. Denominators and the Stickelberger element
    The cost of a map that does not respect integral structures, measured by an eigenspace of a class group.
  • 30. Tate–Shafarevich groups of Tate twists
    The Galois cohomology groups that play this role for a Tate twist, and their orders.
  • 31. Congruence modules
    The denominator at the twisted places: the congruence module of \(\Delta\) is \(\mathbb{Z}/691\).
  • 32. Eisenstein congruences and Selmer classes
    Ribet's argument: a congruence with an Eisenstein series produces an unramified class.
  • 33. Pseudocharacters and the length bound
    The converse direction, bounding a Selmer group by the length of a Hecke algebra modulo its Eisenstein ideal.
  • 34. Black boxes: Galois representations and local–global compatibility
    What a Galois representation remembers at \(p \ne \ell\), and what an automorphic representation gives to compare it with.

Part VI. The construction and the analytic frame

This part states in general, and develops from the Lean code, what Part I treats in its simplest setting.

  • 35. The objects of the construction
    The field and its places, the hermitian spaces and lattices, the chain of lifts, words, tops and nodes, and the period maps: the vocabulary of the chapters that follow, with the smallest instance written out.
  • 36. The analytic frame
    Windows, translates and the auxiliary characters, in general.

Part VII. Divisibility in general

This part states in general, and develops from the Lean code, what Part III treats in its simplest setting.

  • 37. Divisibility at inert primes, in general
    Theta kernels of hermitian lattices in Lubin–Tate coordinates, the valuation estimate, the chain and the Hecke image: the prime-sum and slope bounds in general.
  • 38. The normalized kernel law
    The scalar in the kernel law, as the Lean formalization controls it: one theta function of one CM curve, normalized at the origin, and its fourth tensor power.
  • 39. Frames at odd positions and the unit scalars
    Dual frames at the odd positions of a Frobenius orbit, and why the scalars normalizing the steps are units.
  • 40. Nondegeneracy at an edge
    The condition on one edge of the building that controls the Hecke image, as the Lean formalization states it, checked in the smallest model; why it needs the Frobenius twist.
  • 41. The colength of a Hecke image
    A bound for the colength of the image of a product of Hecke operators, linear in the depth and the denominator, from nondegeneracy at the edges.
  • 42. Gain and loss at an inert prime
    The gain at each Frobenius orbit, the divisibility of the lifts on the Hecke image, and the bookkeeping between them, which the formalization proves on separate branches.

Part VIII. Rank and periods

This part states in general, and develops from the Lean code, what Part II treats in its simplest setting.

  • 43. Slopes, frames and Bessel–Poincaré series
    The slope inequality for the family of period maps, the frame theorem, and near orthogonality in general.
  • 44. Bessel–Poincaré series and their Gram operators
    Poincaré series for Bessel periods on a unitary group, and why their Gram operator is a sum over double cosets.
  • 45. The seesaw as transport of relations
    Seesaw identities in the form the formalization proves: linear relations among the values at one end of the chain hold at the other.
  • 46. The Bessel identity
    Each period map is, up to unitary changes of variable and a scalar, an inner product with a Bessel–Poincaré series.

Part IX. Heights and covolumes

This part states in general, and develops from the Lean code, what Part IV treats in its simplest setting.

  • 47. Heights, the CM period and the auxiliary prime
    The height of each lift through its linear term, the CM period in general, and the count of forms.
  • 48. Counting forms and the critical level
    The two dimensions of the final pairing given exactly by the mass formula, and the auxiliary prime bounded below through critical levels.
  • 49. Slopes of lattices of forms: the good part and descent
    From the covolume of a lattice of forms of a lift to a lower bound for the slope of holomorphic forms, by linear algebra and descent.
  • 50. The CM period and the flat line
    The period in the main term is the Chowla–Selberg period of a CM elliptic curve, entering through the degree of one metrized line.
  • 51. Lower slope bounds on covers
    A lower slope bound uniform in the point and the lift: covers, a change of weight, an effective arithmetic Hilbert–Samuel inequality, and descent.

Part X. Local terms and Selmer groups

This part states in general, and develops from the Lean code, what Part V treats in its simplest setting.

  • 52. Local terms, congruence defects and Selmer windows
    The local terms of each lift, the congruence defects at the twisted places, and the Selmer hypothesis on windows, in general.
  • 53. Local exponents of the lifts
    The local exponents given by formulas, and why their sizes are bounded: a congruence at the terminal prime, rows at the level primes, Minkowski's bound and orbit losses at inert primes.
  • 54. Exceptional twists in cyclotomic towers
    Iwasawa theory along a cyclotomic tower: Ferrero–Washington, a bound for \(H^2\), and the few twists at which the Tate–Shafarevich groups can be large.
  • 55. The minus part, by Gauss sums
    The minus part of the Selmer bound, at every scale, from Stickelberger elements and Gauss sums.
  • 56. The plus part, by congruences
    The plus part of the Selmer bound, from congruences with modular forms case by case, given a bound on fixed layers at the bad primes.
  • 57. Windows of scales
    The Selmer bound on windows as a theorem: a window of scales on which the layers at two and at the bad primes stay small.

Part XI. Assembly

  • 58. Assembly at one scale
    The final inequality at a single scale \(x\): each term of the error traced to the estimate that pays for it, the choice \(m \approx x^{1/2}\), and what must hold uniformly in a window.
  • 59. The accounting in the Lean formalization
    The record the formalization proves at one scale: its further inputs, where the gain and the slopes of the sources come from, the exponent \(9\varepsilon\), and the order in which \(e_D\), \(e_L\), \(\varepsilon\) and the family are chosen.

Side series

One short series stands outside the sequence.

  • S. The sign-only method. These two sheets follow the proof written out in Alderliefste's deposit, which reports that it was produced by an AI model and that no human has checked it. For a lisse sheaf on an open subset of the affine line the same proof has been written out by D. Zhang (arXiv:2611.30749). The weak Lefschetz theorem is now quoted from its published source, Katz's appendix to Affine cohomological transforms, perversity, and monodromy (1993), Corollary A.5; the hyperplane sections used are a problem. So the main line of S1, from the identity to the Riemann hypothesis for curves, rests on Zhang's note and on Katz. Two parts still rest only on the AI-written deposit: the coupling through a finite map \(U \to \mathbb{A}^1\) for an arbitrary affine curve in S1, which the Riemann hypothesis for curves no longer needs, and all of S2 beyond dimension one. Deligne's bound itself is a theorem; what is unchecked is this route to it in higher dimension. The series is a model: the proof of the Riemann hypothesis for $L$-functions does not use it.

    • S2. Deligne's bound by the sign-only method
